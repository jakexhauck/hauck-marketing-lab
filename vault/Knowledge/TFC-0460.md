# When to Fire Sales Reps

## TL;DR
Media Buying Expert sales training on agency sales, closing, and high-ticket deals.

## Content
23. When to Fire Sales Reps.mp4 This question comes up, which is when to fire sales reps. It's unfortunate, but sometimes it does need to happen. So I'm going to refer back to my times when I worked at Grant Cardone's office and what I saw in him. His business, and I'd really encourage that. It works quite well. So at one point when I came in, he had about 25 salespeople, and a lot of them were doing outbound dialing. They were responsible for building their own lead lists, calling those leads, and closing those leads. That's how they were judged. They were also all tracked statistically, meaning every day you came in, there was a giant set on the wall of graphs and statistics. Each one of those graphs was typically the person's sales revenue, outbound dials, connection rate, and whenever those statistics started to fall. That person would be brought in. And they would be asked, 'Hey, why are your statistics declining? Where are we lacking in the training that we need to provide to you? What's causing this?' They would talk about it at the end of every week. Versus if somebody's statistics were good, they would encourage that person to help train other staff. They would ask that person about what they were doing well. They would ask that person what's creating the momentum. They would share those lessons with the organization. Now to be clear, when the person's sales statistics were declining, on Friday specifically, that's when they would bring everybody in. So four times a month, every Friday, they would bring that person into the office and they would ask them, 'What's going on with your statistics?' Or, 'Congratulations on your statistics; keep doing everything you're doing. What's going well?' If two weeks in a row... the person's statistics were going down. They would then let the person know on that second week. That if there was two more weeks of consecutive down statistics, that they were unfortunately going to have to be let go based on their performance. So they'd give the person essentially four weeks of leeway before they would cut the person off. Within those first two weeks, as I mentioned. They would talk about what they needed. If the person said, 'No, everything's fine.' You know, I expect it to turn around. Great. Let's see it. Most of the time, though, in fairness, they'd ask, 'Hey, this is what I'm currently lacking or this is what I'm currently struggling with.' This is where I think I can use some improvement. And that would be provided to them. Up to four weeks was as long as they'd tolerate. Typically, on a month-to-month basis, the bottom 10% of the sales organization would get let go. And that's just kind of how it continued to trend month over month. They ended up with more salespeople by the time I left there. They had about 40-ish by the time that I left. I believe they have more today. The salespeople weren't really... against it. And to be fair, I want to point out that if all of the sales team statistics were trending up, they would not cut the bottom 10%. Typically, there's at least 10% of the sales organization that does have statistics that are trending down. And remember, when you as a business owner are spending money on advertising to generate leads, it is not only a loss of the salesperson losing out, it's a loss of you as the business owner losing out on revenue. Because your salespeople are essentially wasting opportunities. So it's important that you get them out fast. About four weeks on average is what Grant would tolerate. I'd encourage the same for everybody else's organizations. But remember... You can't just guess who's doing good and bad. He tracked all of their statistics in order to judge who is good and who is bad. Don't forget that part.
