How online ads actually work

Stripped to its bones, every ad campaign is the same six-step machine. We'll use a pizza shop as the example, but the same machine runs for a window cleaner, a plumber, or any other local business willing to put a few dollars behind it.

The six pieces of any ad campaign

Every campaign — whether it's running at ten dollars a day or ten thousand — is built from the same six pieces. Memorise them and you'll never feel lost on a strategy call, no matter how much jargon someone tries to bury you in.

Start with budget. You pay Meta — the parent company of Facebook and Instagram — to show your ads to its users. Think of it as paying someone to put flyers on doors, except the someone is a $1.4 trillion machine and the doors are everyone in your zip code. Next comes targeting, which is simply you picking who sees the ad: location, age, a few interest signals if you want them. Meta does the rest of the heavy lifting from there, and it does it embarrassingly well.

The creative is the ad itself — image or video, headline, body copy. This is the flyer. Get this wrong and nothing else matters; get it right and even mediocre targeting will work. A click happens when someone taps the ad. Most people won't, and that's normal — a one-to-three-percent click-through rate is healthy on a cold local audience. A conversion is when the person who clicked actually does the thing you wanted them to do: calls, books, fills out the form, walks in the door. Finally, ROAS — return on ad spend — is what you got back versus what you spent. It is the whole point of the exercise, and the only number a business owner should ever ask about.

ROAS is the only metric that actually matters. Everything else is just a clue about why it's the number it is. — Operating principle, page one

What the funnel looks like on $10 a day

Most of the people who see an ad won't click. Most of the ones who click won't convert. That's the shape of the funnel, not a problem to fix. Here is what a healthy day looks like at the bottom end of the spend spectrum — ten dollars on the table, a few thousand eyeballs, a handful of customers.

$10spent on the day
500–1,000people see the ad Impressions
15–25tap the ad Clicks
3–5turn into leads
1–2become paying customers

If each customer is worth forty dollars or more — and for most local businesses that's a conservative floor — that's eighty dollars back on the ten you spent. An eight-times return, give or take, on a day where you didn't touch the account.

Thirty days of real numbers

Here is what a healthy account actually looks like at the one-month mark. The figures below are pulled from a window-cleaning client running at ten dollars a day, no creative refresh, no fancy audience layering — just the six pieces, set up correctly and left to compound.

$312 Spent
$4,200 Revenue back
13.5× ROAS
31,400 Reached
847 Clicks
47 Leads

Nothing about that month was unusual — it is roughly what the median local account looks like when the offer is sharp and the creative isn't actively working against it. The lesson, such as there is one, is that the machine doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be assembled correctly and then left alone long enough for the numbers to mean something.
